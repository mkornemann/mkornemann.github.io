Job Market Paper

Errors in Aggregation and Adaptive Choice

with Philipp Schirmer

Download Paper (PDF)

Most economic decisions require aggregating multiple attributes into an overall evaluation. We develop and experimentally test a theory where aggregation is error-prone and, in response, attributes are weighted optimally given an environment of likely options. We characterize these decision weights, which generically differ from attributes’ impact on utility, and the bias they induce in choice. When attributes are independently distributed among likely options, higher-variance attributes are over-weighted, and this bias increases in the level of aggregation errors. We test the theory in an experiment by first manipulating between subjects which attribute varies more in choice options, and then comparing choices between options that are identical across subjects. Consistent with the model’s predictions, choice probabilities shift toward the option favored by the high-variance attribute, in our baseline treatments by about 10 percentage points. We also test the effect of aggregation complexity by lowering the number of attributes. Compared to the baseline, this reduces the shift in choice probabilities by about 72%. We apply our model to purchases where base and add-on prices may be displayed separately. A consumer reacts to a short-term change in the display as if it were a price change, while her reaction to a long-run change depends on the details of the environment. Consumer welfare is generally maximized by a fully aggregated price.

Working Papers

Robustly Non-Harmful Information for Biased Learners

Download Paper (PDF)

I examine when robustly beneficial information can be provided to a receiver who also learns from misspecified background sources that are outside the provider’s control. In contrast to information provision for rational receivers, any source can be harmful under certain misspecifications of background sources. I show that the key aspect of the background environment enabling robustly beneficial design is the receiver’s perception rather than the true structure. For any background source structure and design of the provided source, there exists a misspecification under which harm occurs. Consequently, even complete knowledge of the true structure is insufficient and knowledge of the receiver’s perception is necessary. Under complete knowledge of the perception, I demonstrate how to design an information source that is robustly non-harmful and often strictly beneficial, regardless of the true background sources.

Social Responsibility in Secondary Markets

with Marc Kaufmann and Botond Kőszegi

Reject and Resubmit, Journal of Political Economy

Download Paper (PDF)

We study how secondary markets for durable goods interact with consumers’ social-responsibility motives to mitigate environmentally harmful new production. On the positive side, secondary markets may allow responsible consumers to acquire used goods that would otherwise be discarded, reducing premature waste. On the negative side, secondary markets introduce two major harmful forces. First, the possibility of buying used goods and thereby causing less harm can raise the demand of responsible consumers, often increasing the production necessary to serve the market. Second, said demand can increase the price of used goods, encouraging purchases of new goods. These forces imply that if used goods may have negative private consumption value, then secondary markets can enhance or erode the benefits of social responsibility. If, instead, used goods have positive private value, then secondary markets always (weakly) erode the benefits of social responsibility. Importantly, the logic underlying the harmful forces does not apply to new products sold after their production and associated externalities have been sunk. In the unique equilibrium, consumers treat such goods as having the same externality as production.
